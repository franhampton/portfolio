Plain-English AI guide

Why everyone's suddenly talking about AI

A four-minute guide I wrote for business people who are new to AI: no hype, no jargon, just what AI actually is, why it's suddenly everywhere and where it can help at work. Versions of my guides were published on LinkedIn.

My role: Wrote the guide as one of my plain-English AI guides, published on LinkedIn.

What AI actually is

Think of AI as a new hire who read nearly every book, article and manual ever written, and started at your company one hour ago. It writes well, knows a lot and never gets tired or cranky. But it doesn't know your clients, your file names or the unwritten rule that Steve likes his bids formatted a certain way.

It writes a new answer. It doesn't look one up.

Google finds pages other people already wrote. AI writes a brand-new answer, word by word, based on patterns it learned. That's why it can draft, summarize and explain things a search engine can't.

It also means AI can sound confident while being wrong, especially on specific details. This is called a "hallucination." Always double-check anything that matters before you rely on it.

Are all AIs created equal?

Not every AI tool remembers past tasks. Memory means the tool can recall details from earlier conversations, like your preferences or a project you're working on, instead of starting from zero every time. Some tools, like Claude, offer memory as an optional feature you can turn on.

Without it, think of the AI as a specialist who has read an enormous amount and can help you brilliantly in the moment. But once your conversation ends, they walk away remembering nothing about it. The next time you talk, you're meeting them for the first time again.

This isn't new. The last few years are.

Researchers have worked on AI since 1956. What changed recently is that computing power, data and a design breakthrough finally made it good enough for everyday work.

1950s–1990sThe long research phaseDecades of academic progress, mostly in research labs and narrow tasks like chess engines.
2012–2017The building blocks click into placeA 2012 breakthrough in pattern recognition, then a 2017 design (the "transformer") that let AI track context across a whole passage.
2020–2022Scale changes everythingCompanies trained these systems at a scale nobody had tried before, and the results crossed into genuinely fluent text.
2023–todayFrom novelty to normal business toolNow built into how ordinary businesses draft, analyze and manage their day, with a human still checking the output.

Where it's already helping

Construction

Turn a stack of field reports into a one-page summary by job site, in minutes instead of an hour.

Legal and contracts

Flag unusual clauses in a vendor agreement before a lawyer reviews it.

Finance

Compare two spreadsheets and flag every difference over a set dollar amount.

Customer service

Draft a reply to a common question in seconds, then personalize it.

Retail and marketing

Draft a dozen product descriptions in minutes, then edit each for brand voice.

Healthcare admin

Turn scheduling notes and appointment requests into a clean, organized weekly calendar.

Good at vs. still needs a human

Genuinely good at

  • Reading long documents fast
  • Drafting a first version of something
  • Cleaning up messy information
  • Explaining new ideas in plain language
  • Handling repetitive busywork

Still needs a human

  • Anything with real consequences: legal, medical or financial sign-off
  • Judgment calls tied to company history or relationships
  • Checking its own facts
  • Understanding your specific business
  • Owning the final decision

Three straight answers

How do I know it's right?

You don't, automatically. Treat its work like a draft from a smart, brand-new employee: a strong start that still needs a human to check before it goes out the door.

Will it take people's jobs?

It's changing which tasks fill a person's day, the way spreadsheets changed bookkeeping. It removes busywork so people spend more time on judgment calls and relationships.

Is it storing everything I type?

It depends on the tool. Good business AI tools keep conversations private, don't use them to train the AI for other companies, and only see the files or programs you connect. Check the privacy policy before entering anything sensitive, the same rule you'd use for any new software.

Why businesses are moving now

The gap between businesses that have automated their repetitive work and those that haven't is starting to show up in real numbers: how fast a bid goes out, how quickly a question gets answered.

It usually starts small: one tedious task, handed to a tool, checked by a human, refined until it's reliable. The businesses getting the most value aren't the ones using the most AI. They're the ones who picked a few real tasks and stuck with them.

Bottom line. You don't need to understand how AI works to benefit from it, just like you don't need to understand an engine to drive a car. The best first step isn't "adopt AI." It's picking the one task you dread most and seeing if AI can help with just that.
